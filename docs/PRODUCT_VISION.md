# NEUROPLAY — Product vision

**Document type:** Living vision / product philosophy  
**Status:** Direction approved at concept level; feature and UI implementations remain open to revision  
**Not:** A fixed roadmap, a wireframe, a medical reference specification, or a promise that every proposed feature will ship.

## The one-sentence vision

**Make the brain feel discoverable:** turn familiar human experiences into playful questions, then let learners investigate the neuroscience through intuitive interactions, accurate 3D context, and optional layers of depth.

## Why NEUROPLAY should exist

Most people are curious about their own mind long before they know the words *hippocampus*, *synapse*, or *cognitive control*. Traditional resources often begin with terminology, dense diagrams, and definitions. NEUROPLAY begins with a felt experience: remembering, forgetting, attention, habit, emotion, perception, and decision-making.

The goal is not to remove complexity. It is to **make complexity approachable on demand**. Learning should reward curiosity instead of asking learners to prove prior knowledge before they can explore.

A good reference image or paragraph can inform a person; a well-designed playground can help them **form and test a mental model**.

## The learning promise

A visitor with no neuroscience background should be able to:

1. Recognize a familiar question about behavior or experience.
2. Make a low-stakes prediction, without fear of getting it wrong.
3. Manipulate something meaningful—an anatomical view, a variable in a conceptual experiment, a sequence, or a connection.
4. See a short, comprehensible explanation of what the interaction illustrates.
5. Follow a voluntary path toward deeper mechanisms and credible evidence.
6. Leave with one better question and a more accurate mental model.

This is not rote memorization or trivia dressed up with animation.

## Audience and tone

**Primary:** curious beginners and students who enjoy asking *why does my mind do that?*  
**Secondary:** educators, presenters, self-learners, and curious adults who want visually supported explanations.

Do not design only for experts, and do not make the experience childish. The atmosphere should feel **inviting, confident, curious, expressive, and thoughtful**. The interface can be playful while the underlying science remains serious.

The default explanation should work without prerequisite knowledge. Advanced terminology belongs in expandable layers with plain-language definitions.

## Six product principles

### 1. Behavior before anatomy

Start from everyday relevance. Use the brain as an explanatory world, not as an isolated catalog of labels. Anatomy-only free exploration remains possible, but it should not dominate a beginner's first encounter.

### 2. Play is a way to think

Interactions should invite prediction, contrast, experimentation, or discovery—not merely reward tapping a button. A short, thoughtful experiment is more valuable than a dozen decorative animations.

### 3. Beginner first, depth by choice

Use progressively disclosed explanations: **plain-language insight → visual mechanism → optional deeper science → evidence and limitations**. A beginner should not have to scroll through a research paper to understand a phenomenon.

### 4. Biological truth is non-negotiable; explanation level is flexible

Anatomical assets, region names, spatial relationships, and neuroscience statements should be checked and attributed. Illustrative simulations must not imply that their invented numbers are measured brain data. Avoid one-region/one-behavior storytelling where cognition is distributed across networks.

### 5. Immersion should support comprehension

3D, lighting, camera movement, and theme transitions exist to guide attention and reveal relationships. They are not an excuse to sacrifice readability, control, accessibility, or performance.

### 6. Creative evolution without architectural chaos

Make room for surprising design discoveries. New approved decisions replace obsolete assumptions. Preserve working functionality unless the requested change intentionally alters it. Favor reusable structures and small, independently reviewable increments.

## The two-atmosphere identity

**Discovery / learning:** a bright, soft, light-lavender setting, spacious and approachable. This is where questions, explanations, simple mini-games, and invitations live.

**Exploration:** an immersive deep-violet environment that gives realistic 3D anatomy visual focus and a sense of entering a hidden system.

The shift between these is **part of the learning journey**, not merely a conventional light/dark settings toggle. Both environments should still feel unmistakably NEUROPLAY. User comfort, sufficient contrast, and reduced-motion preferences take priority over spectacle.

This is a confirmed **creative direction**, not a requirement for a particular animation, exact color palette, or fixed screen division.

## What NEUROPLAY is not

- Not a diagnostic, treatment, mental-health assessment, or personalized medical-advice tool.
- Not a static anatomy atlas with a quiz tacked on.
- Not a wall of chapters disguised as cards.
- Not a medical workstation or research dashboard intended as the primary beginner interface.
- Not an AI chatbot whose educational value disappears when paid inference is unavailable.
- Not a product that claims precise neural simulations without valid scientific grounding.
- Not a high-pressure, streak-driven learning app that punishes mistakes.

## Editorial stance

- Make clear distinctions between **well-supported consensus**, **useful educational simplification**, and **active scientific uncertainty**.
- Prefer nuanced descriptions: “the hippocampus is important in forming some kinds of new memories” over “the hippocampus stores all memories.”
- Do not imply that the hippocampus alone explains forgetting a person's name; memory encoding, associations, retrieval, attention, and language-related processes all matter.
- Explain unfamiliar terms through an everyday analogy first, then gently correct where the analogy breaks down.
- Link or record meaningful scientific sources for published neuroscience claims.
- Provide explanations for why a prediction was reasonable or why its implications are limited.

## Signs of success

Qualitative questions for review rather than rigid KPI targets:

- Can a curious beginner quickly find something relatable without understanding neuroscience jargon?
- Does at least one interaction change what they understand, not just what they have clicked?
- Can they explore deeper without losing the thread of the original question?
- Is the 3D reveal scientifically honest, comprehensible, and delightful?
- Can a teacher use an experience to start a conversation?
- Can new content be added without rewriting the core interaction framework?

## Creative north star

**“I came because the question sounded like me. I stayed because I could actually explore why.”**
